0Introduction

To size the steering, we need the torque the driver feels at the steering wheel and the force the rack has to carry. We first estimate the steering wheel torque from the tire forces, then turn it into a force on the rack.

1Steering Wheel Torque

In a corner the front tires make a lateral force behind the steering axis. The distance it acts behind the axis is the kinematic (mechanical) trail from the geometry plus the pneumatic trail from the tire, so it makes a torque about the steering axis that tries to straighten the wheels. Dividing by the steering ratio moves that torque to the steering wheel:

T = mV (rT + RP) ayiS

  • T: torque at the steering wheel (N·m)
  • mV: vehicle mass (kg)
  • rT: kinematic trail, also called mechanical trail (m)
  • RP: pneumatic trail (m)
  • ay: lateral acceleration (m/s²)
  • iS: kinematic steering ratio, steering wheel angle over road wheel angle

2Rack Force

The steering wheel torque, T, turns the pinion, which moves the rack. The force on the rack, F, comes from the rack travel per full turn of the steering wheel, c. ARG27 uses a NARRco rack with c = 4.0 in/rev = 0.1016 m/rev (Appendix A).

F = 2πTc

This comes from setting the work in equal to the work out. One full turn of the steering wheel puts in T × 2π, and the rack moving its travel for one turn puts out F × c:

2πT = Fc

The rack’s c is its own steering ratio, so it is already in this equation. The overall steering ratio, iS, is only used in Section 1, to move the torque from the steering axis to the steering wheel.

With a steering wheel torque of 7 N·m:

F = 2π × 70.1016 = 433 N

This ignores friction in the rack and the joints, so anything that has to drive the rack, such as an actuator, should be sized larger than this.

AAppendix: NARRco Rack
