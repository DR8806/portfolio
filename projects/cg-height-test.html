CG Height Test

Test procedure for measuring the CG height of ARG26 with two methods: a side tilt test and a front lift test.

1Purpose

This document outlines how we find the center of gravity (cg) height of ARG26 through a physical test. Although we can make reasonable estimates through CAD, validating the cg height is important because it influences a lot of our load transfer and handling characteristics.

There are two main independent methods to calculate the cg height:

  1. Method A: Side Tilt (Balance) Test. The car is tipped onto the tires of one side until it balances. The balance angle gives the CG height directly.
  2. Method B: Front Lift (Axle Load) Test. The front of the car is lifted while the rear tires sit on scales. The change in rear axle load gives the CG height.

2Method A: Side Tilt (Balance) Test

2.1Theory

A body balances on a pivot when its CG is directly above the pivot. In this test the car is tipped sideways onto the tires of one side, so it rests only on the outer edges of those two tires. The car is slowly rotated until it balances and can be held in place with a couple of fingers, with the crane strap slack. At that point the CG lies on the vertical line through the pivot.

In the car's own frame, the CG sits half the car's width, d / 2, in from the pivot edge and a height h above the ground plane. When the car is rotated by the balance angle θ, the line from the pivot to the CG is vertical, so tan θ = (d / 2) / h.

  1. Install the dummy shocks and dummy cones.
  2. Install the accumulator and bolt it down (ask someone on high voltage).
  3. Make sure the car is on the magnesium rims.
  4. Overinflate the tires to 30 psi.

2.5Equations

The front and rear of the car have different widths, so the width is taken at the lengthwise position of the CG (Figure 2) [3]. The CG sits a distance a behind the front axle, set by the fraction of weight on the rear axle, WR = WRL + WRR:

a = L × WR / W

d = df + (dr − df) × WR / W

CG height above the ground at static ride height:

h = (d / 2) / tan θ

where θ = |θbal − θ0| is the change in the level reading from level to balanced. This assumes the CG is on the car's centerline; averaging tilts onto both sides cancels out a small side-to-side offset.

An error in θ changes h by roughly Δh ≈ (d / 2) × Δθ / sin2θ (Δθ in radians). A 0.2° error gave roughly a 0.1 in error in h for the car measured in [3], so measure the balance angle carefully and repeat it.

2.6Procedure

  1. Complete the pre-test procedure and the measurements before tilting.
  2. Chock the lower-side tires so they cannot slide.
  3. Attach the crane to the raised side and lift slowly. Spotters hold the car at all times.
  4. Keep raising until the car balances on the outer edges of the lower tires, holding in place with a couple of fingers and the strap slack.
  5. Confirm balance with the plumb bob, then record the level reading at the same spot, θbal.
  6. Lower the car back to level. Repeat for three trials.
  7. If safe, repeat three trials tilting onto the other side.

3Method B: Front Lift (Axle Load) Test

In progressMethod B is not finished yet.

3.1Theory

With the rear tires on the scales, the front of the car is lifted by the crane so the car pivots about the rear axle. As the front rises, the CG moves rearward relative to the rear axle, so the rear scales read more weight. The higher the CG sits above the axle line, the larger that increase is. Measuring how much the rear axle load increases for a known change in angle gives the CG height [1], [2].

3.3Pre-Test Procedure

  1. Install the dummy shocks and dummy cones.
  2. Install the accumulator and bolt it down (ask someone on high voltage).
  3. Make sure the car is on the magnesium rims.
  4. Overinflate the tires to 30 psi.
  5. Check each scale with the known weight: less than 0.5 lbf of variance among the scales, and less than 0.5 lbf of error from the known weight, per 50 lb added.
  6. Set the rear scales on the cinder blocks, then level and zero them.
  7. Chock the rear wheels.

3.5Derivation

The car does not need a 50/50 weight split. Let a be the distance from the CG to the front axle and b the distance from the CG to the rear axle, measured along the axle line, so a + b = L (Figure 3). The CG sits a height ha above that line.

With the front raised by an angle α, the horizontal distance from the rear axle to the CG is:

x = b cos α − ha sin α

The crane force F acts at the front axle, a horizontal distance L cos α from the rear axle. Taking moments about the rear axle:

F × L cos α = W × (b cos α − ha sin α)

F = W × b / L − W × (ha / L) × tan α

The rear scales carry the rest of the weight, WR = W − F. Using a + b = L:

WR = W × a / L + W × (ha / L) × tan α

With the car level (α = 0) this is just the static rear weight, WR1 = W × a / L. The weight split only sets that starting reading. Subtracting it leaves the change in rear weight, which depends only on ha:

WRn − WR1 = W × (ha / L) × tan(αn − α1)

3.6Equations

Rear axle weight at each position n:

WRn = WRLn + WRRn

CG height above the axle centerline:

ha = L × (WRn − WR1) / ( W × tan(αn − α1) )

CG height above the ground at static ride height, adding the average loaded radius:

h = ha + (rf + rr) / 2

The level corner weights also locate the CG along the car: a = L × WR1 / W and b = L × WF1 / W.

The change in rear weight is small compared with the total weight, so scale error can become a large part of the result [3]. Use large angles (20° to 40° of change) and average several measurements [4]. The tires also deform under load, lowering the CG slightly, and the value is not exact at speed because of aero loads and tire pressure changes [4].

3.7Procedure

  1. Complete the pre-test procedure and the measurements before lifting.
  2. Lift the front of the car with the crane, keeping the chain vertical.
  3. Hold the car still and record the level reading αn and the rear corner weights WRLn and WRRn.
  4. Repeat at several angles, aiming for about 10°, 20°, 30° and 40° of change.
  5. Lower the car, re-zero the scales, and repeat the full set for a second run.
