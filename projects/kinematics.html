Kinematics

Suspension kinematics design for the ARG26 car, done in OptimumK.

ARG26 uses a double wishbone suspension at all four corners, with push link and rocker actuated dampers and blade style anti-roll bars front and rear.

NoteARG27 does not introduce major kinematics changes. The ARG27 suspension work is focused on rates.

2Kinematics Methodology

Kinematics are chosen by looking at how the suspension behaves at static ride height and how it changes as the car moves through sweeps in OptimumK. The sweeps checked are:

2.1Heave

  • Camber change vs heave
  • Steering angle vs heave
  • Motion ratio vs heave

2.2Roll

  • Camber change vs roll
  • Lateral roll center position vs roll
  • Vertical roll center position vs roll

2.3Steering

  • Left and right steer angles vs steering wheel angle

3Expected Motion

Each sweep needs a range to run over. The ranges come from the motion the car is expected to see on track.

3.1Heave

The heave sweep runs from full compression to full droop. For ARG26 that is 2 in of compression to TBD in of rebound.

3.2Load Cases

The load cases were determined by our unsprung lead, Parker. She compiled racing data from the IMU on the PDU to get the acceleration cases. The IMU does not sit at the CG, so the data is post processed to the CG first with the IMU CG telemetry corrector, which uses:

aCG = aIMU − α × r − ω × (ω × r)

where α is the angular acceleration, ω is the angular velocity, and r is the position of the IMU relative to the CG.

The combined cases apply the listed acceleration both laterally and longitudinally. Bump is vertical only. ARG27 is slightly higher in cornering and accel, and similar in braking.

3.3Pitch and Roll Gradients

With the load cases, the pitch and roll of the car can be estimated by multiplying each acceleration by its gradient. The pitch gradients come from the Pitch sheet of the SU26 Parameters and Analysis spreadsheet: −0.523 deg/g in dive and −0.556 deg/g in squat. The roll gradient is designed to fall between 0.55 and 0.7 deg/g. The maximum, 0.7 deg/g, is used here to get the largest range of motion.

4Bump Steer

4.1OptimumK Sign Convention

  • +Heave = rebound
  • −Heave = compression
  • Positive toe = toe out
  • Negative toe = toe in

4.2ARG26 Bump Steer

On ARG26, the front toe changed along a curve of about 0.7° across the wheel travel, and the rear toe changed linearly at −0.38 deg/in designed and −0.40 deg/in measured.

4.3Zero Bump Steer on ARG27

For ARG27 we changed to zero bump steer, and therefore zero ride steer, at the front and the rear. Toe change in heave adds understeer or oversteer that the driver does not ask for. We do not want that from the kinematics, because we can get the balance we want from elsewhere on the car.

4.4Moving the Toe and Steering Links

As the wheel moves up and down, the A-arms move the outer point of the toe link (rear) or steering link (front) along a path. The link itself swings the outer point along an arc around its inner point. If the two paths do not match, the link pushes or pulls on the upright as the wheel moves, and the wheel steers.

For zero bump steer, the link has to follow the same arc as the path the suspension sets. That means the inner point sits at the center of that arc and the link is the right length, as in Figure 4. On ARG26 the links did not follow that arc, so for ARG27 we moved the inner and outer pickup points of the toe and steering links and reran the heave sweep in OptimumK until the toe change was close to zero across the travel.

5Ackermann

ARG26 used 12% Ackermann. The methodology for choosing the ARG27 Ackermann is in the Ackermann section below.

6Rocker Pickup Points

On ARG27 the rocker pivot moves down 0.2 in, front and rear, to lower the height the rocker needs.

The limit is the bolt head under the rocker. In CAD there is 0.450 in from the rocker hub to the bottom of the clevis (Figure 5), and the bolt head takes up about 0.16 in of that (Figure 6). That leaves 0.29 in of room, so we moved the pivot 0.2 in and kept about 0.09 in of clearance.

Ackermann

The methodology used on ARG27 to determine what Ackermann geometry to design the car to.

0Introduction

The goal is to determine what Ackermann geometry we should design our car to. This document outlines the methodology used on ARG27 to determine an Ackermann percentage.

1Background

1.1What is Ackermann?

Ackermann steering describes the relationship between the steering angles of the inside and outside front tires during a turn.

1.2Defining Our Model and Variables

The analysis models the car in steady-state cornering at a cornering radius R.

Steering angle, δ
The angle between the vehicle's x-axis and the wheel plane.
Attitude (sideslip) angle, β
The angle between the vehicle's x-axis and the velocity vector at a specified point. In a bicycle model, we introduce the front and rear attitude angles βF and βR (see Figure 2). In a full-car model, we introduce four attitude angles, βFL, βFR, βRL, βRR, one for each corner (see Figure 3).
Slip angle, α
The angle between the wheel plane and the wheel's velocity vector. In a bicycle model, we introduce the front and rear slip angles αF and αR (see Figure 2). In a full-car model, we introduce four slip angles, αFL, αFR, αRL, αRR, one for each corner (see Figure 3).

2Pure Ackermann (No Rear Slip)

We still assume steady-state cornering, now with zero slip angle at every tire. Purely geometrically, the inner and outer tires travel on different radii, so to have no slip they must be steered to different angles. These angles can be calculated from the geometry as follows, where L is the wheelbase, T is the track width, and R is the cornering radius.

Inner wheel steering angle:

δin = tan−1( L / (R − T/2) )

Outer wheel steering angle:

δout = tan−1( L / (R + T/2) )

Since L and T are fixed by the vehicle architecture, the only variable that affects the steering angles is the cornering radius R.

Both steering angles decrease as the cornering radius increases. This makes intuitive sense, since a wider turn needs less steering. Because we are interested in Ackermann, the difference between the inner and outer steering angles, we take the difference:

Ackermann steering angle difference:

Δδ = δin − δout

The steering angle difference decreases quickly as the cornering radius increases and approaches 0. This graph may suggest that Ackermann varies a lot with cornering radius. First, though, we should specify the range of cornering radii the car typically operates in. The minimum and maximum values are taken from Endo's report.

The takeaway from this graph is that, still assuming no slip, the steering angle difference for pure Ackermann ranges from 0° to 5°.

3Incorporating Rear Slip Angle

Section 2 assumed the rear tires have no slip angle. In that case the car turns about the geometric center, which sits on the line of the rear axle. Referring back to Figure 3, once the rear tires have a slip angle, the car instead turns about an instant center (IC) that is different from the geometric center. Because the rear wheels are not steered, the rear slip angle moves the IC forward of the rear axle line.

We call the distance along the vehicle's x-axis from the geometric center to the IC, ic.

We now recalculate the front steering angles about the IC instead of the geometric center. The IC is still a distance R from the vehicle centerline, but it now sits L − ic behind the front axle instead of L. Still assuming no slip at the front tires, each front wheel points perpendicular to the line from the IC to that wheel.

Inner wheel steering angle:

δin = tan−1( (L − ic) / (R − T/2) )

Outer wheel steering angle:

δout = tan−1( (L − ic) / (R + T/2) )

With no rear slip angle, ic = 0 and these reduce to the equations in Section 2.

4Incorporating Slip Angles

Section 3 still assumed the front tires have no slip angle. To make lateral force, each front tire has to run at a slip angle, so each front wheel's steering angle is its attitude angle β from Section 3 plus its slip angle. To find the slip angle each front tire wants, we look at the tire data.

5Estimating Ackermann

5.1Defining % Ackermann

Ackermann percentage compares the steering angle difference we want to the steering angle difference of pure Ackermann (Section 2) at the same cornering radius.

%Ack = 100 × Δδtarget / Δδ100%

Δδ100% = tan−1( L / (R − T/2) ) − tan−1( L / (R + T/2) )

  • 100% = pure Ackermann (Section 2)
  • 0% = parallel
  • Negative = reverse Ackermann

5.2Method and Inputs

For a given corner, we find the load on each tire and the slip angle each tire runs at. The rear slip angle places the instant center (Section 3). Each front wheel's steering angle is then its attitude angle β about the instant center plus its slip angle. The difference between the two front steering angles, divided by the pure Ackermann difference at the same radius, gives % Ackermann.

We assume a neutral car at the limit, with every tire at its peak. Figure 3 shows this case: a neutral car in steady-state cornering with equal slip angles at the front and rear. Because the peak slip angle barely changes with load (Section 4), every tire at its peak means every tire at about 11°, so the rear tires also run at about 11°.

5.6Step 4: Rear Slip Angle and Instant Center

From Step 3, the rear slip angle is αR = 11°. The rear slip angle sets how far the instant center moves forward of the geometric center (Section 3):

ic = R × tan(αR) = 5 × tan(11.0°) = 0.972 m

L − ic = 1.549 − 0.972 = 0.577 m

5.7Step 5: Steering Angle of Each Front Wheel

Each front wheel's steering angle is its attitude angle β (Section 1.2, Section 3) plus its slip angle α:

β = tan−1( (L − ic) / (R ∓ T/2) )

δ = β + α

The inside wheel is R − T/2 = 5 − 0.559 = 4.441 m from the instant center, and the outside wheel is R + T/2 = 5 + 0.559 = 5.559 m.

WheelAttitude angle, β+ Slip angle, α= Steering angle, δ
Insidetan−1(0.577 / 4.441) = 7.41°+ 10.8°18.21°
Outsidetan−1(0.577 / 5.559) = 5.93°+ 11.2°17.13°

Δδtarget = 18.21° − 17.13° = 1.08°

5.8Step 6: Convert to % Ackermann

100% Ackermann is the same corner with no rear slip (full L = 1.549 m) and no front slip, using the same distances of 4.441 m and 5.559 m from Step 5:

Δδ100% = tan−1(1.549 / 4.441) − tan−1(1.549 / 5.559) = 19.23° − 15.57° = 3.66°

The target difference is Δδtarget = 1.08° from Step 5, the inside steering angle (18.21°) minus the outside steering angle (17.13°).

%Ack = 100 × 1.08 / 3.66 = 30%

So for a 5 m corner radius, the target is about 30% Ackermann.

What the sweep shows:

  • About 48% Ackermann at the hairpin minimum and 6% at the skidpad radius, crossing to reverse Ackermann at about 8 m.
  • The target drops as the radius grows because the rear slip angle pushes the instant center forward by ic = R × tan(αR). Past about 8 m, ic is longer than the wheelbase, so the instant center sits ahead of the front axle and the front wheels' attitude angles flip toward reverse Ackermann. This means the whole car is crabbing around the corner with its nose pointed in, which only happens when the rear slip angle is larger than tan−1(L/R).
  • At large radii, % Ackermann gets very large because pure Ackermann's angle difference is tiny there (0.14° at 27 m), so a small difference becomes a big percentage. In degrees, the target stays within 0.4° of parallel past the skidpad (Δδtarget column). The tight corners are where the choice of Ackermann matters most.

6.1Flaws and Drawbacks

These apply to both the method in Section 5 and the radius sweep here.

  • Rear slip angle is fixed at 11°. This is the biggest lever. Because ic = R × tan(αR), the instant center moves forward 0.19 m for every meter of radius, which is what makes the target fall so fast. With αR = 6° instead of 11°, the result at 5 m rises from 40% to 70%, at the skidpad from 6% to 51%, and the switch to reverse Ackermann moves from 8 m out to about 15 m. The real rear slip angle has not been measured.
  • Neutral car at the limit. Every tire is assumed to be at its peak at the same time (Figure 3). A real car usually reaches the limit on one axle first. If the car understeers, the rear tires sit below their peak and run less slip angle.
  • Raw tire test data. The TTC data is not scaled to the track. At 200 lbf it peaks at 519 lbf, a friction coefficient of about 2.6, well above what the car pulls on track. It is also one tire at one pressure, 0° inclination angle, cornering only (no braking or drive force), on a test belt.
  • Peak slip angle is hard to pin down. The curves are flat near the top, so the force stays within about 1% of its peak from roughly 9° to 12°. The points were also read off a plot by pixel analysis. The 0.4° difference between the inside and outside front tires in Section 5 is within this uncertainty, but it moves the 5 m result from 40% to 30%.
  • Fixed loads in Section 5. The tire loads assume 1.5 g, a fixed 48% front load transfer split and no aero. The outside front load (235 lb) is past the highest tire data load (200 lbf), so its peak slip angle is extrapolated.
  • Steady-state cornering only. Corner entry, trail braking, corner exit and transient steering are not included.
  • Simplified geometry. The car is rigid with the same track front and rear. Static toe, bump steer, roll steer, compliance steer and how the steering axis (kingpin and caster) changes the wheel angles are all ignored.
  • % Ackermann is compared at the same radius. At large radii, pure Ackermann's angle difference is tiny, so the percentage gets very large even though the target is under half a degree. A real steering linkage sets Ackermann by steering angle, not radius. Past about 10 m, the result is really a static toe question.
  • Not checked against data. None of the slip or attitude angles have been compared with logged data. Yaw rate, speed and steering angle from the car would give the real body slip angle and steering angles to check against.

7Changing Ackermann Geometrically

Ackermann is set by the top-view geometry of the steering linkage: where the steering arm pickup on the upright sits relative to the steering axis, and where the steering rack sits fore and aft.

7.1Front-Steer Layout

On a front-steer layout, the steering rack is ahead of the front axle line.

  • To increase Ackermann: move the steering arm pickup outboard (toward the wheel) or forward, or move the steering rack rearward (toward the front axle line).
  • To decrease Ackermann: move the steering arm pickup inboard (toward the car centerline) or rearward, or move the steering rack forward.

With the pickup outboard of the steering axis, the inside wheel has to turn further than the outside wheel for the same rack travel. On a front-steer car this pushes the pickup toward the brake rotor, so packaging inside the wheel limits how much Ackermann we can get.

7.2Ackermann Across the Steering Range

A real linkage does not give a constant % Ackermann. It changes with steering angle, and moving the pickup or the rack changes both the amount of Ackermann and how it changes across the steering range. Every change has to be checked across the full steering range in OptimumKinematics.

8ARG27 Ackermann

ARG26 used 12% Ackermann. The worked example in Section 5 gives about 30% Ackermann at a 5 m hairpin. The target for ARG27 will be set from the radius sweep in Section 6.

BAppendix: Peak Slip Angle and Ackermann

How the slope of the line of peaks relates to the Ackermann a tire asks for.

Slope ConditionTire BehaviorRequired Geometry Impact
Positive SlopePeak slip angle increases with load The heavily loaded outside tire needs a higher slip angle than the lightly loaded inside tire. Demands Reverse (Anti-) Ackermann: You must steer the outside wheel more than the inside wheel. This is common in high-downforce racing like Formula 1.
Negative SlopePeak slip angle decreases with load The lightly loaded inside tire needs a higher slip angle than the outside tire to reach its peak grip. Demands Positive Ackermann: You must steer the inside wheel more than the outside wheel. This matches traditional low-speed geometric Ackermann.
Zero SlopePeak slip angle is independent of load Both tires reach their maximum cornering capacity at the exact same slip angle regardless of weight transfer. Demands Parallel Steering: Both wheels should be steered at identical angles relative to the chassis to track optimally.
